## ICSE Class 5 English Grammar - Complete Chapter Notes

### Chapter: Subject and Predicate

**1.** In every sentence, the person or thing we talk about is called the Subject. The part of the sentence that tells us what is said about the Subject is called the Predicate. Every complete sentence must have both a Subject and a Predicate, because the Subject tells us who or what the sentence is about, and the Predicate tells us what that person or thing is doing or what is being said about them.

**2.** We can find the Subject in a sentence by asking the question "Who" or "What" before the verb. For example, in the sentence "The postman brings letters," if we ask "Who brings letters?" the answer is "The postman," which is the Subject. The remaining part, "brings letters," is the Predicate. This method of asking "Who" or "What" works for almost every sentence and helps identify the Subject correctly.

**3.** When the Subject is separated from the rest of the sentence, the Predicate remains as everything else in the sentence. The Subject can be a single word, like a name or a pronoun, or it can be a group of words called a phrase or a clause, especially when the Subject is described or explained further by other words.

**4.** In simple sentences, the Subject usually comes first, followed by the Predicate. For example, "Meena goes to school" has "Meena" as the Subject and "goes to school" as the Predicate. Similarly, "My dog plays with me" has "My dog" as the Subject and "plays with me" as the Predicate. In sentences like "Naman likes to ride a bicycle," "Naman" is the Subject and "likes to ride a bicycle" is the Predicate. In "Sneha is drinking apple juice," "Sneha" is the Subject and "is drinking apple juice" is the Predicate.

**5.** Sometimes, the complete Subject includes describing words or phrases that tell us more about the main Subject. For example, in the sentence "The box with books is heavier than the one with clothes," the complete Subject is "The box with books," because the phrase "with books" tells us which box we are talking about. Similarly, in "The stars in the night sky twinkled brightly," the complete Subject is "The stars in the night sky," because "in the night sky" describes which stars we mean.

**6.** In some sentences, the Subject is joined with another word or phrase using "and," which creates what is called a compound Subject. For example, in "His books and notebooks are on the desk," the Subject is "His books and notebooks," since both nouns together are performing the action described in the Predicate. Similarly, "My friend and her sister play the violin beautifully" has a compound Subject, "My friend and her sister."

**7.** Sentences can be of different types, and the way we identify the Subject changes slightly with each type. Declarative sentences are ordinary statements that give information, and in these, the Subject usually comes before the Predicate in a straightforward manner.

**8.** Exclamatory sentences express strong feelings such as surprise, joy, or sadness, and they often begin with words like "What" or "How." To find the Subject in an exclamatory sentence, we first remove the "What" or "How" and rewrite the sentence as a simple statement. For example, "How nice it looks on you!" can be rewritten as "It looks nice on you," where "It" becomes the Subject. Similarly, "What fun those days were!" becomes "Those days were fun," so "Those days" is the Subject. It is important to remember that words like "What" and "How" are never part of the Subject in these sentences.

**9.** Interrogative sentences are sentences that ask a question, and they usually end with a question mark. To find the Subject in an interrogative sentence, we often need to convert the question into a statement first. For example, "Do you know that Asia is the largest continent?" becomes the statement "You know that Asia is the largest continent," making "You" the Subject. However, when the question itself asks about an unknown person, such as "Who broke the mirror?" or "Who brought these beautiful flowers?", the word "Who" itself becomes the Subject, because we do not know the identity of the person being asked about.

**10.** Inverted sentences are sentences where the normal word order is changed, often for poetic or dramatic effect, so that the Predicate or part of it appears before the Subject. To find the Subject in an inverted sentence, we must first rewrite the sentence in its normal order. For example, "Under the tree sat a wise old man" should be rewritten as "A wise old man sat under the tree," making "A wise old man" the Subject. Similarly, "On the burning desk, stood the boy" becomes "The boy stood on the burning desk," so "The boy" is the Subject. Another example is "Out of the bush, came out a snake," which becomes "A snake came out of the bush," making "A snake" the Subject.

**11.** Imperative sentences are sentences that give a command, make a request, or give an instruction, such as "Bring your homework tomorrow" or "Do not speak ill of anyone." In imperative sentences, the Subject is always "You," but this "You" is silent or understood, meaning it is not actually written in the sentence. The entire sentence itself forms the Predicate, since the command is telling an unstated "you" to do something.

**12.** When dividing a sentence into Subject and Predicate, it is important to include all the describing words that belong to the Subject as part of the complete Subject, and not leave them as part of the Predicate. For example, in "A big oak tree stood in a corner of the yard," the complete Subject is "A big oak tree," including the describing words "big" and "oak," while the Predicate is "stood in a corner of the yard."

### Chapter: Adjectives - Degrees of Comparison

**1.** There are three Degrees of Comparison in Adjectives of Quality. They are called the Positive Degree, the Comparative Degree, and the Superlative Degree. These three degrees show how the quality of an adjective changes depending on whether we are describing one thing, comparing two things, or comparing more than two things.

**2.** The Positive Degree of an adjective simply denotes the existence of some quality in the thing or person we are speaking about, without making any comparison at all. For example, in the sentence "Sneha is a tall girl," the word "tall" is in the Positive Degree because no comparison is being made with anyone else. We use the Positive Degree when we are not comparing the thing or person with anything else.

**3.** The Comparative Degree of an adjective denotes a higher degree of the quality than the Positive Degree, and it is used when two objects, people, or things are being compared with each other. For example, in the sentence "Meenu is taller than Sneha," the word "taller" is in the Comparative Degree because two girls are being compared.

**4.** The Superlative Degree of an adjective denotes the highest degree of the quality, and it is used when more than two objects, people, or things are being compared. For example, in the sentence "Meenu is the tallest of the three girls," the word "tallest" is in the Superlative Degree because three girls are being compared together.

**5.** Articles can be used before Adjectives in the Positive Degree. For example, we use "a" before "beautiful," "an" before "honest," and "the" before "brave," as in the sentences "Sonali is a beautiful girl," "Ankit is an honest man," and "Shudhanshu is the brave boy who jumped into the river to save a drowning child." In these Positive Degree sentences, no comparison is made with any other person.

**6.** In sentences using the Comparative Degree, no Article is used before the Comparative form of the adjective. Instead, the word "than" is used after the Comparative Degree to introduce the second thing being compared. For example, "Sonali is smarter than Rupali" and "Ankit is wiser than Salman" both compare one person with another using "than."

**7.** In sentences using the Superlative Degree, the definite Article "the" is used before the Superlative form of the adjective, because the sentence is emphasising the quality of one person or thing in the highest order compared to all others in the group. For example, "Sonali is the smartest girl in the class" and "Ankit is the wisest player in his team" both use "the" before the Superlative Degree.

**8.** The formation of the Comparative and Superlative Degrees follows several different rules depending on the adjective. The most common rule is that many Adjectives form their Comparative Degree by adding "-er" to the Positive Degree, and their Superlative Degree by adding "-est" to the Positive Degree. This rule applies to a large number of common adjectives describing size, age, speed, temperature, and other simple qualities.

**9.** If the Positive Degree of an adjective ends in the letter "-e," then only "-r" is added to form the Comparative Degree, and only "-st" is added to form the Superlative Degree, instead of adding the full "-er" and "-est" endings. This is because adding a full "-er" or "-est" to a word already ending in "-e" would create an awkward double "e" in the word.

**10.** If the Adjective in the Positive Degree is a word of one syllable that ends with a single Consonant, then the last Consonant of the word is doubled before adding "-er" and "-est" to form the Comparative and Superlative Degrees. This doubling rule helps to keep the pronunciation of the word correct and consistent with English spelling patterns.

**11.** Some Adjectives of the Comparative Degree and Superlative Degree are formed not by changing the ending of the word, but by adding the words "more" and "most" before the Adjective. This rule generally applies to longer adjectives that have more than two syllables, since adding "-er" or "-est" to such long words would make them difficult to pronounce.

**12.** If the Adjective in the Positive Degree ends in the letter "-y," and this "-y" has a Consonant letter before it, then the "-y" is changed into "-i" before adding "-er" and "-est" to form the Comparative and Superlative Degrees. However, if the "-y" has a Vowel letter before it instead of a Consonant, then the "-y" is not changed into "-i," and "-er" and "-est" are simply added directly to the word.

**13.** There are some Adjectives that do not follow any of the regular rules described above while forming their Comparative and Superlative Degrees. These are called Adjectives with irregular Comparatives and Superlatives, and their forms must be learned individually because they do not follow a predictable pattern.

**14.** Among these irregular adjectives, the word "far" has two different Comparative and Superlative forms depending on the meaning intended. When "far" refers to physical distance, its Comparative form is "farther" and its Superlative form is "farthest." When "far" refers to movement or a more abstract sense of extent, its Comparative form is "further" and its Superlative form is "furthest."

**15.** Similarly, the word "old" also has two different sets of Comparative and Superlative forms depending on the context in which it is used. When "old" is used to describe age in general, its Comparative form is "older" and its Superlative form is "oldest." However, when "old" is used specifically to describe a family relation, such as an older brother or sister, its Comparative form is "elder" and its Superlative form is "eldest." For example, "Hari is older than me. He is my elder brother. Prateek is the eldest of all." This shows that "older" is used for general age comparison, while "elder" and "eldest" are used specifically for describing family relationships. Another example is "This house is older than that building. It is the oldest house in the street," which shows "older" and "oldest" being used for age rather than relation.

**16.** One important rule to remember while using Adjectives in the Comparative and Superlative Degrees is that double Comparatives and Superlatives should never be used. This means we should never combine "more" with an "-er" ending, or "most" with an "-est" ending, on the same word, because this creates an incorrect grammatical structure. For example, saying "Shahnaj is more richer than Salma" is incorrect, and the correct form is "Shahnaj is richer than Salma." Similarly, saying "Ramlal is the most oldest man in the village" is incorrect, and the correct form is "Ramlal is the oldest man in the village."

### Chapter: Forms of Verbs

**1.** We use different forms of Verbs in different tenses. There are three forms of Verbs in English grammar, and these are called the Present form, the Past form, and the Past Participle form. The Present form, also called the First Form, is used to describe an action happening now or in general. The Past form, also called the Second Form, is used to describe an action that has already happened. The Past Participle form, also called the Third Form, is used together with helping verbs like "have" or "has" to describe a completed action.

**2.** There is a group of Verbs that remain exactly the same in all three forms, meaning the Present, Past, and Past Participle forms of these verbs are all identical, with no change in spelling at all. This means the same word is used whether we are talking about the present, the past, or a completed action with a helping verb.

**3.** There is another group of Verbs that remain the same in the Simple Present form and the Past Participle form, but have a different Simple Past form. This means the first and third forms of these verbs look identical, while the second form, used for simple past actions, is spelled differently.

**4.** There is a third group of Verbs that have three totally different forms, meaning the Present, Past, and Past Participle forms are all spelled differently from one another. These verbs must be memorised individually since there is no simple pattern connecting their three forms, and they change completely as the tense changes.

**5.** There is a fourth and very large group of Verbs, generally called regular verbs, that remain the same in the Simple Past form and the Past Participle form, but have a different Simple Present form. This means that for these verbs, once you know the Past form, you already know the Past Participle form as well, because they are identical. This is the largest and most common group of verbs in English, and most of them form their Past and Past Participle by simply adding "-ed" or a similar ending to the Present form.

### Chapter: Vocabulary - Antonyms with Prefixes

**1.** An Antonym of a word is a word that has the opposite meaning to that word. Antonyms are important in vocabulary because they allow us to express the reverse or contrary meaning of an idea using a related but different word.

**2.** Many Antonyms in the English language are formed by adding Prefixes to the original words, rather than by using a completely different word. A Prefix is a small group of letters that comes at the very beginning of a word and changes its meaning when added to it.

**3.** There are several common Prefixes used specifically to form Antonyms, and each Prefix generally means "not" or "the opposite of" when attached to a word. These Prefixes include "dis-," "il-," "im-," "in-," "ir-," "mis-," "non-," and "un-," and each of these is used with different types of words depending on the spelling and sound of the original word.

**4.** The Prefix "dis-" is used to form Antonyms of certain words by attaching it to the beginning of the word, and it generally reverses the meaning of the original word or shows a lack of the quality described.

**5.** The Prefix "il-" is used to form Antonyms specifically of words that begin with the letter "l," because this Prefix changes its spelling to match the first letter of the word it is attached to, making pronunciation smoother.

**6.** The Prefix "im-" is used to form Antonyms of certain words, particularly those beginning with the letters "m" or "p," since this Prefix also adjusts its spelling to blend more naturally with the sound of the original word.

**7.** The Prefix "in-" is used to form Antonyms of many other words that do not begin with "l," "m," "p," or "r," and it is one of the most commonly used Prefixes for creating opposite meanings.

**8.** The Prefix "ir-" is used to form Antonyms specifically of words that begin with the letter "r," following the same pattern of spelling adjustment as "il-" and "im-," so that the Prefix matches the first sound of the original word.

**9.** The Prefix "mis-" is used to form Antonyms that specifically show an action done wrongly, incorrectly, or badly, rather than simply showing a general opposite meaning. Words formed with "mis-" usually suggest that something has gone wrong in the way an action was carried out.

**10.** The Prefix "non-" is used to form Antonyms that show a complete absence of a particular quality or category, rather than simply the opposite of a quality, and it is often used with nouns to indicate that something does not belong to a certain group or category.

**11.** The Prefix "un-" is one of the most common and widely used Prefixes for forming Antonyms in English, and it can be attached to a wide variety of adjectives and other words to create their opposite meanings.

**12.** When forming an Antonym using a Prefix, it is important to choose the correct Prefix for each specific word, since different words require different Prefixes even though they may seem similar. There is no single rule that applies to every word, so each Prefix and word combination must be learned individually as part of building strong vocabulary skills.
