# ICSE Class 5 English Grammar - Easy Pointwise Notes

## Chapter: Subject and Predicate

**1.** The person or thing we talk about in a sentence is called the Subject.

**2.** The part that tells what is said about the Subject is called the Predicate.

**3.** Every sentence must have both a Subject and a Predicate.

**4.** To find the Subject, ask "Who" or "What" before the verb.

**5.** For example, "Who brings letters?" tells us that "The postman" is the Subject.

**6.** Whatever is left after removing the Subject becomes the Predicate.

**7.** The Subject can be one word, like a name, or many words, like a phrase.

**8.** In simple sentences, the Subject usually comes first and the Predicate comes after.

**9.** Sometimes describing words are part of the Subject, not the Predicate.

**10.** For example, in "The stars in the night sky twinkled brightly," the full Subject is "The stars in the night sky."

**11.** Two Subjects joined by "and" make a compound Subject.

**12.** For example, "My friend and her sister" is a compound Subject.

**13.** A Declarative sentence is a normal statement giving information.

**14.** An Exclamatory sentence shows strong feeling and often starts with "What" or "How."

**15.** To find the Subject in an Exclamatory sentence, remove "What" or "How" first and turn it into a statement.

**16.** For example, "How nice it looks on you!" becomes "It looks nice on you," so "It" is the Subject.

**17.** "What" and "How" are never part of the Subject.

**18.** An Interrogative sentence asks a question.

**19.** To find the Subject, turn the question into a statement first.

**20.** For example, "Did she complete her work?" becomes "She completed her work," so "She" is the Subject.

**21.** If we do not know who did the action, then "Who" itself becomes the Subject.

**22.** For example, in "Who broke the mirror?" the Subject is "Who."

**23.** An Inverted sentence has the Predicate placed before the Subject.

**24.** To find the Subject, rewrite the sentence in normal order first.

**25.** For example, "Under the tree sat a wise old man" becomes "A wise old man sat under the tree," so the Subject is "A wise old man."

**26.** An Imperative sentence gives a command, request, or instruction.

**27.** In Imperative sentences, the Subject is always "You," but it is silent and not written.

**28.** The whole sentence becomes the Predicate in Imperative sentences.

**29.** Always include all describing words as part of the complete Subject.

## Chapter: Adjectives - Degrees of Comparison

**1.** There are three Degrees of Comparison for Adjectives of Quality.

**2.** They are called Positive Degree, Comparative Degree, and Superlative Degree.

**3.** Positive Degree simply shows a quality without any comparison.

**4.** Comparative Degree shows a higher quality and is used when two things are compared.

**5.** Superlative Degree shows the highest quality and is used when more than two things are compared.

**6.** Articles "a," "an," and "the" can be used before Adjectives in the Positive Degree.

**7.** No Article is used before the Comparative Degree.

**8.** The word "than" is used after the Comparative Degree.

**9.** The Article "the" is always used before the Superlative Degree.

**10.** Many Adjectives form the Comparative Degree by adding "-er" and the Superlative Degree by adding "-est."

**11.** If the Positive Degree ends in "-e," only add "-r" and "-st."

**12.** If the Adjective has one syllable and ends in a single Consonant, double the last Consonant before adding "-er" and "-est."

**13.** Some longer Adjectives form the Comparative and Superlative by adding "more" and "most" before the word instead of changing the ending.

**14.** This rule of "more" and "most" is mainly used for Adjectives with more than two syllables.

**15.** If the Adjective ends in "-y" with a Consonant before it, change the "-y" to "-i" before adding "-er" and "-est."

**16.** If the "-y" has a Vowel before it, do not change the "-y."

**17.** Some Adjectives are irregular and do not follow any of the above rules.

**18.** These irregular forms must be learned by heart, one by one.

**19.** The word "far" has two meanings: for distance, it becomes "farther" and "farthest"; for movement, it becomes "further" and "furthest."

**20.** The word "old" also has two meanings: for age, it becomes "older" and "oldest"; for family relation, it becomes "elder" and "eldest."

**21.** Never use double Comparatives or double Superlatives together.

**22.** For example, do not say "more richer"; simply say "richer."

**23.** Similarly, do not say "most oldest"; simply say "oldest."

## Chapter: Forms of Verbs

**1.** Verbs change their form depending on the tense being used.

**2.** There are three forms of Verbs: Present Form, Past Form, and Past Participle Form.

**3.** The Present Form, or First Form, is used for actions happening now or generally.

**4.** The Past Form, or Second Form, is used for actions that already happened.

**5.** The Past Participle Form, or Third Form, is used with helping words like "have" or "has."

**6.** Some Verbs remain exactly the same in all three forms, with no spelling change at all.

**7.** Some Verbs remain the same in the Present Form and the Past Participle Form, but change in the Past Form.

**8.** Some Verbs have three completely different forms, and each one must be learned separately.

**9.** A large group of Verbs, called regular Verbs, remain the same in the Past Form and the Past Participle Form, but are different from the Present Form.

**10.** Most regular Verbs form their Past and Past Participle by adding "-ed" or a similar ending.

## Chapter: Vocabulary - Antonyms with Prefixes

**1.** An Antonym is a word that means the opposite of another word.

**2.** Many Antonyms are made by adding a Prefix to the beginning of a word.

**3.** A Prefix is a small group of letters placed before a word to change its meaning.

**4.** The Prefix "dis-" is added to certain words to make their opposite meaning.

**5.** The Prefix "il-" is used mainly for words that start with the letter "l."

**6.** The Prefix "im-" is used mainly for words starting with "m" or "p."

**7.** The Prefix "in-" is used for many other words that do not start with "l," "m," "p," or "r."

**8.** The Prefix "ir-" is used mainly for words that start with the letter "r."

**9.** The Prefix "mis-" is used to show that something was done wrongly or incorrectly.

**10.** The Prefix "non-" is used to show a complete absence of something.

**11.** The Prefix "un-" is one of the most common Prefixes and can be used with many different words.

**12.** Each word takes only one correct Prefix, so every word and Prefix combination must be learned carefully.
